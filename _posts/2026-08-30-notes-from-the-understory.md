The first plants we specify for an interior rarely survive. This is not a confession; it is a method. A planting scheme is a hypothesis about light, water, soil and human behaviour, and hypotheses need to be tested.

Indoor planting is often treated as decoration: a fern in a corner, a tree in a lobby, a green wall installed by a specialist and forgotten by everyone else. We are interested in the opposite — planting as a maintenance practice, a light calculation and a long conversation with failure.

## Start with the light, not the plant

Before we choose a species, we measure. We log light levels through the day, in winter and summer, at the height of the intended planting. A spot that looks bright to the eye can be a cave to a plant. A corridor that seems gloomy may hold enough diffuse light for ferns and mosses if the ceiling is high and the walls are pale.

Then we choose from a small palette of plants that tolerate inconsistency: ficus, philodendron, aspidistra, ferns, moss. Not because they are fashionable, but because they forgive us.

> A plant is a schedule. If you cannot keep the schedule, choose a different plant.

## Soil is infrastructure

Most indoor planting fails at the root. The container is too small, the drainage is wrong, or the soil is a bag of something that has never met a living organism. We design planting beds as we design floors: with layers, movement and access for repair.

At Fern House, the winter garden has a raised bed with a gravel reservoir and a slow wick. It is watered once a week, sometimes less. The plants have been in place for four years. Two species have died. Three have spread beyond their allotted space. The garden is better for both.

## The understory is the room

The understory is the layer of a forest that lives in the shade of taller trees: ferns, mosses, small shrubs, things that grow slowly and close to the ground. It is the layer that makes a forest feel cool and quiet. It is also the layer that works best indoors.

We plant the understory first — the ground cover, the low ferns, the moss — and let the taller plants arrive later. This is the opposite of how most interiors are styled, where the big statement tree comes first and the floor is an afterthought. The understory gives a room its temperature. It is what you feel before you notice what you are looking at.

## Failure is part of the palette

We keep a record of every plant that dies. The record includes the light, the soil, the watering schedule and the person responsible. It is not a punishment. It is how the next planting gets better.

A garden that never fails is a garden that has not been tried. The point is not to keep everything alive. The point is to learn the room well enough that most things thrive, and to design the room so that the ones that do not can be replaced without drama.
