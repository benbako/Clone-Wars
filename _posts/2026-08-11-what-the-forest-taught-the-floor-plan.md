A forest is not a collection of trees. It is an arrangement: a canopy that filters light, an understory that holds moisture, a floor that decomposes and returns. Nothing in it is wasted, and nothing is finished. When we are stuck on a floor plan, we go for a walk.

The lesson is not “bring nature inside.” It is structural. Forests organize themselves around light, edge and shelter. So do good buildings.

## Clearings

A forest is mostly shade, interrupted by clearings. The clearing is where things happen: where light reaches the floor, where seedlings compete, where animals gather. A clearing is not empty space. It is a concentration of possibility.

In a house, the clearing is the room that does the most work: the kitchen table, the stairwell, the window seat. We try to give each home one true clearing — a space with more light, more height, more generosity than the program strictly requires. Everything else can be efficient. The clearing should not be.

## Edges

The most fertile part of a forest is its edge, where the canopy opens and two systems meet. Edges are messy, layered and full of life. Buildings have edges too: thresholds, balconies, garden walls, the strip between inside and outside.

We spend a disproportionate amount of time on these strips. A deep threshold with a bench and a place to put wet shoes does more for a house than an extra square metre of living room. A balcony that is wide enough for two chairs changes how a family uses the evening. The edge is where the building meets its weather, and where the people meet each other.

> The plan is not a diagram of rooms. It is a diagram of relationships.

## Shelter

Forests also teach shelter. A large tree does not shelter evenly; it creates a dry patch on one side, a windbreak on another, a pool of shade that moves. Shelter is specific.

The same is true of a home. The best seat in a room is rarely in the middle. It is against a wall, under a lower ceiling, near a window but not in the glare. We design for these small territories: a corner that holds you, a niche for a desk, a stretch of wall for a bed. The plan should offer a range of shelters, not one correct place to sit.

## The floor

Finally, the forest floor: the layer that receives everything and turns it back into soil. In a building, this is the ground plane — the floor, the garden, the storage, the service. It is the least photographed part of architecture and the most important.

A floor that is easy to clean, warm underfoot, and connected to the garden changes daily life more than a dramatic ceiling. A garden that can absorb rainwater and return it to the soil is infrastructure, not decoration. The floor is where the building touches the world.

We do not copy forests. We borrow their logic. Light, edge, shelter, return. Four words that have improved more floor plans than any mood board.
