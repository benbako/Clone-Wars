Every material keeps a record. Timber remembers the years of rain before it was felled, then the saw, the plane, the hand that oiled it. Lime plaster remembers the trowel. Stone remembers the quarry, and then the weather.

We are interested in materials that are willing to show this memory. A surface that looks new forever is a surface that has stopped listening.

## The library of small failures

Our material library occupies one wall of the Lisbon studio: offcuts of oak, chestnut and cedar; tiles of lime plaster at different ages; stone samples with one polished face and one raw edge; a jar of soil from every site we have worked on. The samples are not pristine. They are scratched, stained and sun-bleached, because that is how they will live.

Idris, who runs the library, has a rule: nothing enters the shelf until it has spent a month by a window. We want to see how it behaves under real light, not showroom light. Some samples fail beautifully. A pale plaster turns the colour of weak tea. A softwood darkens around the knots. A stone develops a line that no drawing could predict.

## Repair as a design language

The most radical thing a building can do today is age well. That means designing joints that can be opened, surfaces that can be patched, and finishes that do not require a specialist to maintain.

At Moss Courtyard, the timber floor is laid in narrow boards with a visible gap. When a board is damaged, it can be lifted with a chisel and replaced. The repair leaves a slight difference in tone — a scar, not a flaw. Over time, the floor has become a map of the studio's life: a darker patch where a desk stood for three years, a lighter strip where the sun reaches in winter.

> A material that can be repaired is a material that has been given a future.

## Sourcing close to home

We try to source within two hundred kilometres of a site. This is not purity for its own sake. It is a way of keeping the material's story legible. A stone from a valley an hour away carries the colour of that valley's weather. A chestnut from the hills above the site carries the same rain that falls on the roof.

There are exceptions, of course. Some sites ask for a material that must travel. When that happens, we ask why. The answer is usually interesting, and occasionally it changes the design.

## Designing for the first scratch

Clients often ask how to keep a surface looking new. The honest answer is that they cannot, and that this is the point. A home is not a showroom. It is a place where hands rest on tables, where wine is spilled, where children draw on the floor.

We design for the first scratch. If a material looks better after it, we use it. If it looks worse, we choose something else. That is the whole test.
